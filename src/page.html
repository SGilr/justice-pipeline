England and Wales · 2000 to 2026

More police, less crime, fuller prisons

Officer numbers fell by a seventh after 2010 and were rebuilt by 2023. Survey crime kept falling through both. The strain in the system now sits downstream, in the courts, on remand and in prison cells.

02 · Relative change

What grew, what shrank, from the same starting line

Every series rebased to 100 in a chosen year. Police funding was cut by about 15% in real terms after 2010 and has only recently recovered. From 2019 the police uplift added about a fifth to officer numbers. The Crown Court caseload and the remand population roughly doubled over the same period, while survey crime fell.

03 · Police and crime

Did more officers mean less crime?

Each dot is a year: across is officers per 100,000 residents, up is crime measured by the victim survey. Crime fell steeply while officer density rose in the 2000s, and kept falling while it shrank after 2010. The national picture gives no simple dose and response; the research on targeted, hot spot and problem-oriented policing is where the effect shows.

04 · Capacity and strain

The pipeline since the uplift began

Change from 2019 to the latest figure at each stage. The tick marks the 2019 level; the bar shows where each stage is now. Recorded crime is flat over the period, so the strain is not simply more crime. Courts that had cut sitting days in 2018/19 then lost a year to the pandemic and months to the 2022 barristers' strike; more officers and a recovering charge rate sent more cases in. Cases waited longer, more defendants were held on remand, and the prison estate ran out of room.

06 · Children and disproportionality

Fewer children locked up; a widening ethnic gap among those who are

The youth justice system is the success story of the period: first-time entrants down over 90% since 2007 and custody down by more than four fifths. The fall has been slower for children from ethnic minority backgrounds, who are now the majority of those held. In adult policing and prisons, Black people remain heavily over-represented.

    08 · Data and notes

    Read before citing

    Table view of every series

    ≈ marks an estimate or reconstructed value. Blank means no comparable figure.

    1. Officers and PCSOs from 2007, police recorded crime, the crime survey, charge rates, court caseloads and every force-level figure are read directly from the published source tables. Points marked ≈ (officers and PCSOs before 2007, remand before 2018, children in custody before 2010, stop and search before 2018, and the funding index) are approximate reconstructions; verify them against the source tables before citation.
    2. Police recorded crime excludes fraud and computer misuse and starts in 2002/03, when the National Crime Recording Standard took effect. It is shaped by recording practice: the National Crime Recording Standard (2002), the HMIC crime data integrity inspection (2014) and the 2023 counting-rule change for conduct crimes all shift the series. Its post-2014 rise mostly reflects better recording, not more crime.
    3. The Crime Survey (CSEW) excludes fraud and computer misuse here for comparability. The 2000 point is the 1999 calendar-year survey. The 2020/21 telephone survey is not comparable and is omitted. Lower response rates led to loss of accredited status in 2023, so treat the latest points with care.
    4. Officer figures are full-time equivalent at 31 March in the 43 territorial forces. Rates use ONS mid-year population; 2025 and 2026 use projected population.
    5. Prison and remand populations are June snapshots except 2025 (September). Crown Court and magistrates' caseloads are end of December except 2026 (end of March), from the Ministry of Justice criminal court statistics tables published June 2026.
    6. Police funding is an index of total real-terms spending (central grant plus council tax precept). 2000/01, 2005/06, 2010/11 and 2014/15 come from IFS analysis; 2023/24 is placed at 5% below 2009/10 following the Institute for Government; 2025/26 applies the Home Office's stated 4.1% real-terms rise in force funding. Intermediate years are interpolated and the sources use different price bases, so read it as shape, not exact level.
    7. Charge rates are the Home Office share of all recorded crimes assigned a charge or summons outcome, by the year the crime was recorded. Recent years can still rise as investigations close.
    8. Stop and search ratios use Census 2011 denominators to 2022 and Census 2021 thereafter, which mechanically lowered the ratio.
