England and Wales · 2000 to 2026

More police, less crime, fuller prisons

Officer numbers fell by a seventh after 2010 and were rebuilt by 2023. Survey crime kept falling through both. The strain in the system now sits downstream, in the courts, on remand and in prison cells.

02 · Relative change

What grew, what shrank, from the same starting line

Every series rebased to 100 in a chosen year. Police funding fell by 13% in real terms between 2010/11 and 2014/15 and recovered from 2019. From 2019 the police uplift added about a fifth to officer numbers. Over the same period the Crown Court caseload doubled and the remand population rose by two thirds, while survey crime fell.

03 · Police and crime

Did more officers mean less crime?

Each dot is a year: across is officers per 100,000 residents, up is crime measured by the victim survey. Crime fell steeply while officer density rose in the 2000s, and kept falling while it shrank after 2010. The national picture gives no simple dose and response; the research on targeted, hot spot and problem-oriented policing is where the effect shows.

04 · Capacity and strain

The pipeline since the uplift began

Change from 2019 to the latest figure at each stage. The tick marks the 2019 level; the bar shows where each stage is now. Recorded crime is flat over the period, so the strain is not simply more crime. Courts that had cut sitting days in 2018/19 then lost a year to the pandemic and months to the 2022 barristers' strike; more officers and a recovering charge rate sent more cases in. Cases waited longer, more defendants were held on remand, and the prison estate ran out of room. Remand has eased since mid-2025, falling 13% in the year to June 2026.

05 · The release valve

Early release, again: the progression model from 1 October 2026

From 1 October 2026 most people serving a standard determinate sentence are released automatically at the one-third point, rather than at 40% (the SDS40 change of September 2024) or halfway. Some serving four years or more for more serious offences move from two thirds to half. After a review ordered by the new Prime Minister, Andy Burnham, the government excluded rape, certain child sexual offences and grooming offences, unlawful killing and historic indecent assault. Extended and life sentences are unaffected. Existing prisoners are being released in ten tranches by sentence length between October 2026 and June 2027.

Why the relief is likely to be short-lived

Moving the release point is a one-off cut to the stock of prisoners: everyone already past the new point leaves together, and from then on people simply leave a little earlier. It does nothing to the inflow. Remands, charges and sentence lengths keep pushing the population up, and the Ministry of Justice expects demand to grow by about 3,000 places a year without further action.

The Ministry's own January 2026 projections show the pattern. With the Sentencing Act, central demand dips to about 86,300 in May 2027 and then climbs again by around 1,500 to 2,000 places a year, reaching 95,900 by November 2032. In the high scenario demand exceeds supply in November 2027, through 2028 and again from November 2030. Those projections also predate the August exclusions, under which about 1,400 people who would have left on a first day stay in custody, so the real saving will be smaller.

SDS40 followed the same course: headroom rose to about 2,800 places after the October 2024 tranche and was back below 1,500 within four months.

Criticisms

  • Victims. The Victims Commissioner and Domestic Abuse Commissioner asked for a delay in July 2026. The Domestic Abuse Commissioner remains concerned that most domestic abuse perpetrators are still eligible.
  • Scope. The opposition argues the exclusions leave many violent offenders, including domestic abusers, eligible at one third; amendments to exclude sexual and violent offences were defeated.
  • "Earned" progression. Release at one third is the default; critics note prisoners must break rules to lose it.
  • Probation. Released people move onto caseloads in a service with 5,559 FTE probation officers against a target of 7,114 (June 2025).
  • Durability. On the Ministry's own projections the system returns to the margin within a few years unless inflow, remand or sentence lengths change.

Safeguards the government points to

  • Offence exclusions, keeping about 1,400 first-day releases in custody, alongside extended and life sentences.
  • Intensive supervision after release, new restrictive licence conditions and wider electronic monitoring; GPS monitoring for 12 months for those convicted of rape or serious child sexual offences.
  • Up to 84 added days for breaches of prison rules, up from 42.
  • Up to £700 million more for probation and community services by 2028/29, and a national helpline for affected victims.
  • About 14,000 new prison places by 2031, of which 2,900 had been delivered by January 2026.

07 · Children and disproportionality

Fewer children locked up; a widening ethnic gap among those who are

The youth justice system is the success story of the period: first-time entrants down 90% since 2008/09 and custody down by more than four fifths. The fall has been slower for children from ethnic minority backgrounds, whose share of those held rose from 40% to around half between 2014/15 and 2020/21. In adult policing and prisons, Black people remain heavily over-represented.

    1. Every series is read from the published source tables listed below. Points marked ≈ are official figures on a slightly different basis: prison population for 2000 and 2001 (annual averages, not June), the crime survey for 2000 (calendar 1999), the funding index for 2000/01 and 2005/06 (IFS spending figures spliced to the Home Office series at 2010/11), and child first-time entrants where the series moves from financial to calendar years in 2014. Officer numbers before 2003 are not shown because no comparable published series was available.
    2. Police recorded crime excludes fraud and computer misuse and starts in 2002/03, when the National Crime Recording Standard took effect. It is shaped by recording practice: the National Crime Recording Standard (2002), the HMIC crime data integrity inspection (2014) and the 2023 counting-rule change for conduct crimes all shift the series. Its post-2014 rise mostly reflects better recording, not more crime.
    3. The Crime Survey (CSEW) excludes fraud and computer misuse here for comparability. The 2000 point is the 1999 calendar-year survey. The 2020/21 telephone survey is not comparable and is omitted. Lower response rates led to loss of accredited status in 2023, so treat the latest points with care.
    4. Officer figures are full-time equivalent at 31 March in the 43 territorial forces. Rates use ONS mid-year population estimates; 2026 uses mid-2025. The Home Office gives 236 officers per 100,000 for March 2026 (Police workforce tables, Table H4) because it divides by the mid-2024 population; this page uses the latest estimate, mid-2025, which gives 235. The force map follows the Home Office and uses mid-2024.
    5. Prison and remand populations are 30 June snapshots (MoJ tables A1.1 and the prison population data tool), with June 2026 from the MoJ prison population tables at 30 June 2026 (Table 1.Q.1); the pipeline uses June 2026. Crown Court and magistrates' caseloads are end of December except 2026 (end of June), from the Ministry of Justice criminal court statistics tables for April to June 2026 (published 24 September 2026, Tables C1 and M1). The 2000/01 and 2005/06 points are IFS estimates of total police spending in 2014/15 prices, scaled to the same base; they show direction rather than an exact level.

The release valve section uses HMPPS weekly population and capacity bulletins (2024 to 2026), the MoJ analytical report on progression model tranche release volumes (August 2026), SDS40 and ECSL transparency data, and the MoJ Annual Statement on Prison Capacity 2025.
